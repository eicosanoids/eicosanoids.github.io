Most eicosanoids are finally shortened two carbons at a time, in the peroxisome. The route is drawn once here, and the family maps point to it. It starts outside the peroxisome: the acid is joined to coenzyme A by a synthetase on the endoplasmic reticulum, and isolated peroxisomes shorten a prostaglandin only when microsomes are added (Schepers 1988). No paper names the gene of that synthetase, so the enzyme carries no symbol.

Acyl-CoA oxidase makes the first cut and gives hydrogen peroxide, and cells of patients without the enzyme cannot shorten an epoxide of arachidonic acid (Fang 2000). The next step is the D-bifunctional protein: patients without it, and patients with infantile Refsum disease, pass raised leukotriene E4 and its omega-carboxy form in urine while the shortened products are undetectable (Ferdinandusse 2002).

The last cut and the way into the peroxisome are drawn green. The same patient work rules out two candidates that are often drawn: ACAA1, because patients lacking it have normal leukotriene profiles, and the transporter ABCD1, normal in X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy. SCP2 is drawn because nothing better is known, and no transporter has been identified at all.

What takes this route, and what does not: prostaglandins (a Zellweger infant given labelled PGF2alpha passed the un-shortened diacid instead of the usual tetranor metabolite, Diczfalusy 1991), leukotrienes, 12-HETE and 15-HETE but not 5-HETE, and the epoxides of arachidonic acid, which keep their epoxide while they are shortened, so for them this route competes with the hydrolase instead of following it. Mitochondria can do it too, without carnitine, but patients who cannot use the mitochondrial route still shorten their eicosanoids normally.

## What this map means

These are the steps a mediator takes after it has acted, the routes by which it leaves the network. They are not new branches of production. Where a product keeps its activity, this text says so, because the route alone does not tell whether a signal has ended.
