Cytochrome P450 ω-hydroxylases of the CYP4A and CYP4F families (CYP4A11, CYP4F2, CYP4F3) make 20-HETE, a vasoconstrictor in the kidney and the vessel wall. Other P450s hydroxylate arachidonic acid one to four carbons from its end. 19-HETE comes from several P450s. Compared side by side among the human enzymes, CYP2C19 makes most of it, followed by CYP1A1, for which it is the main product, CYP1A2 and CYP2E1 (mostly the 19S form). CYP2J2, mainly an epoxygenase, makes about a fifth as much as CYP2E1, and CYP2U1 adds 19-HETE in brain and thymus. 18-HETE comes from CYP4F8 and CYP4F12, which hydroxylate the ω-3 carbon, and from CYP2E1 as the 18R form. CYP1A1 also makes 17- and 16-HETE in smaller amounts, and CYP4F2 makes 16-HETE.

## Relevance to disease

Where to look at this pathway from, for disease. 20-HETE, the main product of this map, has a receptor whose loss protects: truncating variants of GPR75 found in large-scale human sequencing lower body mass index and halve the odds of obesity, and mice without the receptor resist weight gain on a rich diet (Akbari 2021). The inactivation map of this atlas shows what happens next, and it matters here: oxidation to the dicarboxylic acid turns 20-HETE into a vasodilator and a PPAR agonist, so that step changes the signal instead of ending it.
