Platelet-activating factor (PAF, 1-O-alkyl-2-acetyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine) is made in the same step that releases arachidonic acid: cytosolic phospholipase A2 alpha cuts alkyl-arachidonoyl phosphatidylcholine into arachidonic acid and lyso-PAF, which an acetyltransferase turns into PAF. Its receptor PTAFR was cloned from guinea pig lung by its response in frog oocytes (Honda 1991) and then from human leukocytes (Kunz 1992). PTAFR works through Gq, phospholipase C, inositol trisphosphate and calcium, and also through other G proteins (Honda 2002). The calcium signal activates cytosolic phospholipase A2 alpha again, so PAF and the eicosanoids made with it reinforce each other in mast cells, neutrophils, eosinophils and platelets.

## Relevance to disease

Where to look at this pathway from, for disease. The enzyme that destroys PAF has the best human genetics here. A missense mutation in PLA2G7 abolishes plasma PAF acetylhydrolase activity in about four per cent of Japanese people and is enriched among asthmatics, worst in those with two copies (Stafforini 1999, Stafforini 1996), and the same allele is over-represented in abdominal aortic aneurysm (Unno 2002). In human anaphylaxis, PAF rises with severity while that enzyme activity falls, and it was lowest in fatal peanut anaphylaxis (Vadas 2008).

The same enzyme is also the atlas's clearest case of a marker that is not a target: its activity predicted cardiovascular events in 14,500 patients with stable coronary disease, and lowering it by 65 per cent with darapladib changed no outcome (Wallentin 2016). On the receptor side, mice with extra PAF receptor are hyperreactive in the airway and die more readily of endotoxin, so receptor amount alone sets the severity (Ishii 1997).
